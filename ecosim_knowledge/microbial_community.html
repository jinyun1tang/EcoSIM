01 / THE OVERALL REPRESENTATION

Functional diversity, connected through pools and fluxes

EcoSIM represents microbial communities as biomass pools with functional identities and guild traits. Each population changes its activity in response to temperature, water conditions, nutrient status, available substrates, and—in aerobic pathways—oxygen supply. The populations interact mainly by consuming and producing shared chemical pools, rather than through a prescribed matrix of species-to-species interactions.

What organisms can do

Functional groups select metabolic pathways: aerobic respiration, denitrification, fermentation, methanogenesis, methane oxidation, nitrification, N₂ fixation, and cyanobacterial photosynthesis.

How much they can do

Guild biomass and physiological traits set potential rates. Resource concentrations, competition factors, transport limits, and donor caps constrain those rates.

What they become

Accepted substrate uptake supports maintenance, reserves, and structural biomass. Mortality recycles part of C/N/P and sends the remainder to microbial residue and humified material.

The central coupling is energy supply → maintenance allocation → growth-associated uptake → reserve and structural updates. These stages are distributed across group-specific catabolic modules and shared anabolic routines. A change in one stage must be reflected in the associated substrate withdrawals, respiratory products, biomass gains, and competition diagnostics.

Scope. This document describes the implemented process structure, not proof that every reaction or long-term budget is correct. The recent PAR correction is present. The starvation-respiration redox issue is documented by TODOs and remains unresolved; the proposed O₂-only patch was reverted.

02A / INTERACTIONS AMONG THE THREE LIVE POOLS

Reserves supply both built-biomass pools; turnover recycles material back

Each guild has three live C/N/P compartments, not three interacting species. iLbiom_kinetic=1, iLbiom_struct=2, and iLbiom_reserve=3. The same organization applies to heterotrophic/mixotrophic and autotrophic-family biomass. In the heterotrophic family it is repeated for each organic complex K.

M = 1

Kinetic / labile biomass

Represents the fraction used to infer metabolic capacity. Its C determines the active-biomass proxy; its N:C and P:C ratios constrain activity. It receives growth from reserves, incurs maintenance demand, and loses material through ordinary and starvation mortality.

M = 2

Structural / recalcitrant biomass

Receives a separate share of reserve allocation and has its own turnover coefficient. Only the portion supported by kinetic biomass is counted as active structural biomass for maintenance. It remains a live compartment, distinct from dead microbial residue.

M = 3

Reserve / nonstructural biomass

Stores C/N/P available for allocation. It receives net assimilated C, nutrient uptake and recycling, and supplies both kinetic and structural growth. Reserve nutrient status also controls mineral uptake/release and recycling fractions.

Growth arrows transfer C/N/P. Recycling arrows return C/N/P from ordinary mortality, but only N/P from the currently implemented starvation-recycling route. The unrecycled share leaves the live pools. These arrows summarize within-guild transfers, not priming among complexes.

1. Kinetic biomass controls the active portion of structural biomass

StageBGCEnvironCondition does not simply sum all three pools to determine metabolic capacity. For a guild, write C₁ and C₂ for kinetic and structural C, and f₁ and f₂ for FL(1) and FL(2). Omitting nonnegative guards for readability:

Active-biomass proxy: A = C₁ / f₁
Active structural C: C₂,active = min(A × f₂, C₂)
Active structural N: N₂,active = N₂ × C₂,active / C₂   (zero when C₂ is negligible)

OMActHeter/OMActAutor store A; OMC2/OMC2Autor and OMN2/OMN2Autor track the active structural amounts. The corresponding fraction is FOM2/FOM2Autor. Thus loss of kinetic biomass can reduce metabolic capacity and active structural maintenance even while some structural biomass survives. This calculation changes activity diagnostics, not the stored mass of M2.

Kinetic N:C and P:C ratios also set the nutrient-status scalars (FCN/FCP and their autotrophic equivalents). In these staging equations each scalar is bounded between 0.5 and 1 before their minimum is used. Nutrient effects elsewhere in the model are additional constraints; this particular activity multiplier is not a complete nutrient-feasibility test.

2. Reserve allocation builds kinetic and structural biomass in parallel

The two growth transfers are prepared from the current reserve pool. With C₃ the reserve C and E the temperature/water growth multiplier:

G_C,total = E × OMGR × max(0, C₃)
G_C,1     = FL(1) × G_C,total
G_C,2     = FL(2) × G_C,total

For X = N or P, when C₃ is positive:
G_X,M = max(0, X₃) × min(FL(M), G_C,M / C₃)

These correspond to NonstX2stBiomHeter and NonstX2stBiomAutor. The autotrophic expressions write the same nutrient relation as the minimum of a fraction of reserve nutrients and the C-transfer rate times the reserve X:C ratio. Final N/P transfers may be reduced by the reserve-availability reconciliation before biomass is updated.

There is no direct M1 → M2 “maturation” transfer in these allocation routines. Both receive material from M3. Here the phrase reserve-to-structural growth often refers collectively to construction of M1 and M2, even though only M2 carries the specific enum name iLbiom_struct.

Illustrative allocation, not default parameters. If C₃=10, E×OMGR=0.1 per model step, and FL=(0.3,0.7), the prepared C transfers are 0.3 to M1 and 0.7 to M2. Reserve C decreases by 1 before net assimilation and recycling credits are included. With reserve N=1 and P=0.1, the corresponding proposed nutrient transfers are N=(0.03,0.07) and P=(0.003,0.007), subject to final availability checks.

3. Uptake and recycling replenish reserves

For heterotrophic C, the net current-step input CGROMC is DOMUptk4GrothHeter(C) − RespGrossHeter − denitrification respiration. Its value already accounts for the pathway’s metabolic carbon expenditure. For cyanobacteria, uptake includes photosynthetic C and the respiratory term includes the implemented photosynthate costs. Autotrophic-family updates use analogous accounting with their group-specific C source.

Organic N/P uptake, mineral uptake or release, and N₂ fixation enter the reserve nutrient budgets. They are not credited directly as newly constructed kinetic or structural biomass. LimitReserveNutrientTransfers checks the combined reserve supply against structural nutrient transfers and mineral release.

Ordinary mortality takes C/N/P from M1 and M2. The fractions represented by RkillRecycOM* return to M3; their elemental fractions depend on reserve composition and reserve-to-kinetic C status through CCC, C3C, CNC, CPC, and RCC*. Recycling is therefore part of physiological allocation, not a fixed universal fraction for every element. Unrecycled material enters the residue/humus routes.

4. Maintenance and starvation do not form a simple “drain reserves first” rule

Heterotrophic maintenance demand is based on kinetic N and active structural N, with temperature, pH, and density modifiers. Primary catabolism and, where available, denitrification are allocated to maintenance before growth. The model does not first subtract an arbitrary maintenance demand directly from C₃ and then consume M1/M2 only after C₃ reaches zero.

Instead, a maintenance deficit relative to realized metabolic supply triggers withdrawal from M1 and M2, capped by tissue remaining after ordinary mortality. The recycled N/P are credited to M3. The recycled C is currently credited to CO₂ rather than M3; the redox-budget limitation described in issue 1 still applies. The remaining C/N/P go to residue/humus. Do not interpret all starvation recycling as a complete C/N/P return to reserves.

5. Pool bookkeeping and timing

For a within-guild update, omit priming, external redistribution, and numerical guards, and let D denote ordinary mortality, S starvation withdrawal, Q ordinary recycling, T starvation N/P recycling, G construction, U net metabolic C input, and I net nutrient input. The bookkeeping is:

For each element X and M = 1, 2:
ΔB_X,M = G_X,M − D_X,M − S_X,M

Reserve carbon:
ΔC₃ = U_C − (G_C,1 + G_C,2) + (Q_C,1 + Q_C,2)

Reserve N or P:
ΔX₃ = I_X − (G_X,1 + G_X,2)
      + (Q_X,1 + Q_X,2) + (T_X,1 + T_X,2)

Starvation CO₂-C credit = T_C,1 + T_C,2  [currently not returned to C₃]

Here TC denotes the carbon counterpart of the starvation-recycling flux. Its CO₂ credit is in addition to the current-substrate respiratory costs already included in UC. These equations describe the transfer logic; they do not imply that the unresolved starvation respiratory chemistry is balanced.

Growth and mortality fluxes are prepared before the final *AnabolicUpdate calls. Consequently, current-step uptake is not simply added to reserves and then immediately used to recompute all construction rates in the same call. Priming can also move biomass between organic complexes before the final update. The late N/P reserve check reconciles availability, but is not a new physiological iteration.

04 / EXECUTION AND COUPLING

One hourly update has several distinct stages

The standalone driver runs hourly forcing and hydrology before MicrobeModel; plant processes, soil chemistry, transport, and later redistribution follow. Microbial routines therefore prepare reactions against a staged environment, while some physical replenishment and final tracer updates occur later.

1 · STAGEEnvironment and states

Send layer pools, concentrations, prior demands, temperature, water conditions, and PAR into the box model. Derive active biomass, nutrient-status factors, effective water volume, and substrate shares.

2 · PROPOSEMetabolic fluxes

Run heterotrophs by K and group, then autotrophs. Group routines calculate catabolism; their wrappers also prepare nutrient exchange, anabolic allocation, and mortality fluxes.

3 · UPDATEOrganic matter and biomass

Apply chemical denitrification, priming, hydrolysis and decomposition-product redistribution, sorption, then autotrophic and heterotrophic biomass updates and colonization.

4 · EXPORTBudgets and transport

Aggregate gas, nutrient, residue, and organic-matter transfers. Return states and fluxes through the API; physical transport and chemistry apply their corresponding updates.

05 / CATABOLISM → ANABOLISM

Respiration supplies a budget; uptake and growth must reconcile with it

Potential, allocated, and realized rates are different quantities

Potential metabolic rates depend on active biomass, guild traits, substrate saturation, environmental response, and nutrient status. Competition factors reserve a portion of a shared resource. Donor caps and electron-acceptor limitations then restrict the realized reaction. Aerobic O₂ routines use shorter gas/water substeps, microbial diffusion geometry, and gas–liquid exchange; their actual/potential uptake ratio is an oxygen limiter, with 1 meaning no O₂ limitation.

For common aerobic organic-C pathways, potential DOC or acetate respiration is bounded by an allocated donor amount multiplied by that donor’s respiratory fraction. The subsequent conversion from respiration to total uptake must use the matching donor-specific fraction. Using an averaged yield for individual donors can violate one donor’s allocation even if the summed C budget looks reasonable.

Maintenance is reserved before growth

CalcRespMaintHeter derives maintenance demand from biomass N, temperature, pH, and density modifiers. GatherHeterotrophRespiration compares that demand with realized primary respiration. Denitrification-supported respiration can fill a remaining maintenance deficit through ReserveDenitrifMaintenance, using a yield conversion to the primary respiratory-C basis. Only the residual capacity supports growth-associated uptake or N₂ fixation.

Simple one-donor illustration:
Rm = min(M, R);   Rg = max(0, R − M)
U = Rm + Rg/η;   net substrate C to reserves = U − R

Here η is a respiration fraction, not carbon-use efficiency; η=0.4 implies 0.4 units respired per unit of growth-associated uptake in this simplified case. Maintenance uptake earns no growth yield. The implemented formulas additionally distinguish DOC and acetate, denitrification, and N₂-fixation allocations.

Reserves connect uptake to structural growth

DOMUptk4GrothHeter and DOMuptk4GrothAutor carry elemental uptake, but their C meaning depends on the group. Organic heterotroph uptake must debit DOC/acetate; cyanobacteria also credit fixed CO₂-C; autotrophic-family carbon may originate from CO₂ or methane according to the implemented pathway. These array names alone do not identify a universal donor.

Structural growth draws from the reserve compartment. For heterotrophs, the potential C transfer is proportional to GrowthEnvScalHeter × OMGR × reserve C, partitioned by FL(M). N and P accompany allocation according to reserve availability and the implemented stoichiometric controls. The model therefore has a reserve-mediated allocation step; uptake is not simply instantaneous new structural biomass.

At the final biomass update, LimitReserveNutrientTransfers reconciles N/P growth withdrawals and mineral release against reserve supply, including uptake and recycling. This matters because priming can alter pools between flux preparation and the final update. It is a local safeguard, not a whole-network C/N/P/redox solver.

Cyanobacteria: photosynthate is a supply

Fixed C supplies maintenance first; the residual can support growth and N₂ fixation. Photosynthetic O₂ offsets respiratory demand before external O₂ uptake; unused O₂ is released. Additional photosynthate respiration for N₂ fixation has a corresponding adjustment to the net O₂ budget.

Each layer receives PAR_RAD_incident at its upper interface before its microbial biology runs. Updated cyanobacterial biomass then contributes to the optical depth used to shade deeper layers, with transmission sfexp(−τ). This follows the intended surface-to-depth update. The microbial call still runs at night with zero PAR.

The litter and bare-soil streams already include surface-cover weighting. Only the litter stream attenuates through litter; both streams attenuate through soil. Their transmitted sum divided by cell area supplies the next layer. Incident exposure differs from both layer-mean irradiance and absorbed light; see the cyanobacterial light guide.

Autotrophic pathways: energy and C differ

Nitrifiers derive energy from inorganic N oxidation while acquiring biomass C separately. Methanotrophs must account for methane used in both energy metabolism and biomass. Hydrogenotrophic methanogens require H₂ for methane formation and biomass synthesis.

A single generic “respiration C” variable cannot, by itself, determine every substrate debit or product. Group-specific stoichiometry remains essential.

06 / MORTALITY AND RETURN TO ORGANIC MATTER

Ordinary turnover and starvation are distinct routes

Ordinary mortality

Mortality removes C/N/P from kinetic and structural biomass, using compartment rates, environmental response, and density modifiers. In the heterotrophic formula, SPOMX = sqrt(GrowthEnvScalHeter) × SPOMC(M) × SPOMK(M), with withdrawal capped by compartment biomass.

Element-specific recycling returns part to reserves. The unrecycled part is partitioned between humified material and microbial residue. Residue subsequently becomes a substrate for hydrolysis and microbial uptake.

Maintenance-deficit mortality

A remaining maintenance deficit triggers additional structural withdrawal. Ordinary mortality is subtracted first when determining the available donor biomass, preventing the two withdrawals from independently consuming the same tissue.

N/P in the recycled fraction return to reserves. The current code instead emits its recycled C directly as CO₂. The rest is routed to residue/humus. This direct C-respiration shortcut is the unresolved redox issue below.

Withdrawn biomass element
  = recycled element + unrecycled element

Ordinary turnover: recycled C/N/P → reserves
Starvation:        recycled N/P   → reserves
                   recycled C     → CO₂  [redox accounting unresolved]

Unrecycled element
  = humified transfer + microbial-residue transfer

EHUM controls the humified share of microbial litterfall. Soil humification feeds the humus complex; surface-litter humification can be routed to the underlying soil. Autotrophic residues require distribution among receiving organic complexes because their live biomass lacks a K axis. The destination weights must sum to one whenever material is transferred.

Mortality is an ecosystem feedback. It reduces future catalytic capacity while returning substrate and nutrients to the community. Recycling changes nutrient retention in living biomass, and residue/humus partitioning changes substrate accessibility and persistence. Correct elemental closure of this partition does not guarantee correct respiratory chemistry.

Sources: GatherHetertrophAnabolicFlux, HeterotrophAnabolicUpdate, AggregateTransfOMBioResdue; autotrophic mortality and AutotrophAnabolicUpdate.

08 / MAJOR IMPROVEMENT NEEDS

Separate confirmed gaps from design choices

Confirmed unresolved issue

1. Starvation respiration lacks pathway-specific redox accounting

Both anabolic updates add RMaintDefcitRecycOM*(ielmc,...) to CO₂ without a matching additional respiratory-reactant budget. Biomass C is debited, so the immediate problem is not carbon creation. It is respiration without the corresponding O₂ demand for aerobic organisms, or the appropriate substrates and reduced products for anaerobic pathways.

A complete correction must separate mortality from realized respiration, constrain reaction fluxes by available reactants, and keep unrespired carbon in organic pools. Facultative organisms need coordinated aerobic/anaerobic allocation. Do not apply an external O₂ charge to all organisms. The two source TODOs deliberately preserve this issue for a coordinated solution.

Sources: HeterotrophAnabolicUpdate, TODO near RCO2ProdHeter; AutotrophAnabolicUpdate, TODO near RCO2ProdAutor.

Architectural limitation

2. Competition does not guarantee a globally feasible reaction set

Previous-demand shares and biomass fallbacks provide an inexpensive ecological allocation rule. However, independently floored shares can sum above one. Adding a new guild or donor pathway requires manual changes to competing-demand sums, fallbacks, donor caps, products, and API accounting.

Local caps improve behavior but do not establish a universal guarantee across C, nutrients, gases, band/nonband pools, and coupled reactions. Keep ecological allocation separate from a common check that all accepted reactions can be supported simultaneously.

Explicit source TODO

3. H₂ allocation remains sequential

H2GSX combines dissolved H₂ with a fermentation supply estimate. Hydrogenotrophic guilds withdraw methane- and biomass-associated demand from the remaining budget in loop order.

This avoids independent reuse within that loop but gives earlier guilds priority. The source explicitly calls for an H₂ competition factor. A shared allocation and a declared policy for same-step cross-feeding would make behavior less sensitive to guild ordering.

H2MethanogensCatabolism

Numerical limitation

4. Deferred donor debt is not accepted-flux correction

SubstrateDribbling holds a donor pool nonnegative and carries unmet withdrawal forward as debt. The signed inventory is pool minus debt; a nonnegative displayed pool can coexist with substantial outstanding demand.

This mechanism does not retroactively scale already credited biomass or products to the available donor. Monitor debt explicitly and reconcile the whole reaction before applying products when strict instantaneous feasibility is required.

SubstrateDribbling; gas source/sink application

Coupling approximation

5. Staged rates and sequential updates need convergence tests

Some kinetics use staged concentrations and previous-hour competition, while hydrolysis, priming, biomass updates, and transport occur in a prescribed order. Some cross-feeding is explicitly credited within the hour, as for H₂; other exchanges wait for later pool updates.

This is a numerical design, not proof of a bug in every case. Test timestep sensitivity and guild-order sensitivity, and specify which products can fund same-step consumption.

Representation and calibration

6. Interface exposure and activity proxies simplify microsites

Cyanobacterial photosynthesis uses incident PAR at the layer’s upper interface. This is an exposure assumption: representing biomass distributed throughout a thick layer would require its depth distribution and light response to be considered together. Sequential shading by updated biomass is the intended column coupling, not a confirmed defect. Layer-level gas/water conditions also simplify microbial spatial heterogeneity.

Hydrolysis and colonization use activity proxies rather than an explicit community-wide enzyme allocation budget in these routines. Evaluate whether finer surface layers, subgrid redox environments, or explicit enzyme costs are needed for the intended application.

Validation priority

7. Test reaction and community invariants

A build or finite-output smoke run cannot verify the chemistry. Tests should track C/N/P, electron equivalents, and every donor, including deferred debt, while varying group counts and competing pathways.

Prioritize no-O₂/no-NOx starvation, mixed DOC/acetate uptake, methane plus biomass demand, band/nonband separation, litter–soil nutrient access, and cyanobacterial day/night transitions.

A direction for a scalable solver

  1. Describe each reaction explicitly: donor pools, products, elemental/redox stoichiometry, potential rate, and energetic role.
  2. Allocate shared resources: keep the ecological rule—demand, biomass, kinetic affinity, or another justified choice—explicit.
  3. Accept a feasible reaction set: apply consistent limits across all donors and scale each reaction’s reactants and products together.
  4. Allocate accepted energy and carbon: maintenance first, then growth and N₂ fixation, with an explicit policy for mortality and recycling.
  5. Apply transfers once: export matched source/sink fluxes and verify state-plus-flux invariants before and after transport.

This is a proposed development direction, not a description of a solver already present. It should preserve useful ecological controls in EcoSIM while reducing the need to patch each new donor–consumer combination separately.
